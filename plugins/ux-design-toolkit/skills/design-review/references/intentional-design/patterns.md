# Conditional pattern catalogue

These are **review prompts, not banned styles**. The repeated record format is deliberate: it makes comparable information easy to retrieve. The records do not claim equal severity, equal evidence strength, or equal explanation length. Pattern names are neutral retrieval labels; interpretations and tests are the supplied research synthesis, not established diagnostic criteria.

A screenshot can motivate a question. It cannot establish authorship, computed contrast, interaction behavior, performance, or actual user difficulty. Open the original interface when available. Sources below support relevant principles, not a validated 13-feature detector.

## P01 — Palette and state meaning

**Dimension:** visual language

An inherited visual palette is being mistaken for a product-specific direction. The color combination alone reveals nothing about authorship or quality.

**Legitimate use.** A warm palette can be an explicit brand choice, a setting for photography, or an intentional emotional register. Shared semantic colors can also support a product family.

**Investigate when.** The generator applies a preferred palette despite approved brand tokens, or uses amber interchangeably for primary actions, warnings, and decoration without enough differentiation.

**Review test.** Compare the output with the actual brand brief and interaction states. Test contrast and whether important states can be understood without color. Distinguish a visual preference from a demonstrated ambiguity.

**Recommended response.** Preserve a suitable palette. Repair conflicting semantic roles and failed contrast. When brand direction is genuinely open, explore references relevant to the audience rather than replacing beige with another fashionable default.

**Protect this counterexample.** An accessible cream-and-amber interface that fits its brief is a retain decision. Do not demand saturated colors or dark mode just to avoid resemblance.

**Research basis:** [S02](sources.md#s02), [S11](sources.md#s11), [S36](sources.md#s36).

## P02 — Typographic emphasis

**Dimension:** typography

Typographic emphasis can be formulaic when every headline emphasizes an arbitrary final word.

**Legitimate use.** A contrasting typeface can establish an editorial voice, quotation, language distinction, brand signature, or meaningful emphasis. Its job can be expressive rather than purely functional.

**Investigate when.** Emphasis changes the apparent meaning, makes the key message harder to read, or is applied regardless of content. A decorative word displaces the information people need first.

**Review test.** Read the headline aloud with the styled word stressed; does that match the intended message? Inspect the heading outline independently of typography and test realistic line breaks and zoom.

**Recommended response.** Choose emphasis around the intended reading or retain it as an explicit brand convention. Reduce competing emphasis when it obscures the message. Preserve semantic headings.

**Protect this counterexample.** Italics need not encode a unique data category to be legitimate. A recognizable editorial signature is an acceptable purpose.

**Research basis:** [S05](sources.md#s05), [S28](sources.md#s28).

## P03 — Secondary labels

**Dimension:** readability and hierarchy

Subordinate visual treatment is being confused with permission to make information difficult to perceive.

**Legitimate use.** An eyebrow, timestamp, category, or secondary attribute can help orientation when it is readable and has an appropriate relationship to surrounding content.

**Investigate when.** A consequential warning, form instruction, unit, or data limitation is made faint or tiny; repeated eyebrow labels add no distinction and compete with useful headings.

**Review test.** Ask what the label enables a person to understand. Check contrast, scaling, text spacing, real devices, and the label’s relationship to controls. Do not infer a CSS size or conformance result from a compressed screenshot.

**Recommended response.** Promote essential content. Remove redundant labels when they do no useful or expressive work. Use typography tokens and validation appropriate to the actual medium.

**Protect this counterexample.** WCAG does not impose one universal minimum font size. A caption is not a failure merely because it is smaller than the title.

**Research basis:** [S02](sources.md#s02), [S03](sources.md#s03), [S04](sources.md#s04), [S05](sources.md#s05), [S14](sources.md#s14).

## P04 — Workflow stages

**Dimension:** task modeling

A presentation template is dictating the model of a real workflow.

**Legitimate use.** A workflow genuinely containing three meaningful stages can be presented that way. Progressive disclosure can also simplify a long task when its dependencies and remaining effort remain understandable.

**Investigate when.** The design hides required work, forces independent activities into a sequence, or describes a branching process as a guaranteed linear journey.

**Review test.** Map actual inputs, outputs, dependencies, resumability, and exceptions before counting screens. Compare what the diagram promises with what the service actually requires.

**Recommended response.** Use a truthful stage summary, checklist, task list, or branching flow as appropriate. Distinguish high-level stages from the number of underlying actions.

**Protect this counterexample.** Three stages can legitimately contain several substeps when the distinction is explicit. Do not replace every three-step flow with a longer one.

**Research basis:** [S12](sources.md#s12), [S13](sources.md#s13).

## P05 — Card grouping

**Dimension:** grouping and components

Containers are being added independently of the relationships and operations in the information.

**Legitimate use.** Repeated cards can work well for a collection of peer entities, previews, media items, or separately actionable concepts. Many cards can remain meaningful when the underlying collection warrants them.

**Investigate when.** Nested containers imply unrelated levels of importance; scattered attribute cards make row-to-row comparison difficult; a border suggests an action or category that does not exist.

**Review test.** Name the entity or relationship represented by each boundary. Ask whether the task is browsing, comparing, ranking, editing, or monitoring. Compare a representative card layout with a list or table using actual content.

**Recommended response.** Retain entity cards where useful; flatten redundant wrappers; use alignment, typography, lists, or tables when those better support the operation. Audit responsive and accessible behavior for the chosen representation.

**Protect this counterexample.** A large catalogue of genuinely similar products is not slop because every product uses a card. Empty-space reduction is not always an improvement.

**Research basis:** [S15](sources.md#s15), [S18](sources.md#s18).

## P06 — Familiar icons

**Dimension:** recognition and iconography

Generic icon slots are filled even when the symbol contributes little or implies unsupported meaning.

**Legitimate use.** A familiar icon paired with clear text can support scanning, action recognition, or a well-established brand vocabulary.

**Investigate when.** A shield implies protection the product does not provide, a bolt is used for unrelated actions, or six icon slots produce six invented benefits. Ambiguous icon-only controls obscure actions.

**Review test.** Ask a representative reader what the icon or control means without prompting the desired answer. Check accessible names and whether adjacent text makes the icon redundant rather than contradictory.

**Recommended response.** Keep useful conventions. Label ambiguous controls, remove misleading symbolism, and select icons for actual concepts rather than choosing concepts to fill slots.

**Protect this counterexample.** More original icons are not automatically more understandable. An obvious labeled search symbol may be exactly right.

**Research basis:** [S16](sources.md#s16), [S29](sources.md#s29).

## P07 — Product imagery and implied capabilities

**Dimension:** product representation

Interface fragments create the appearance of software sophistication without demonstrating a relevant capability.

**Legitimate use.** An actual screenshot, clearly labeled concept, visual explanation, or stylized product illustration can establish what a tool does. Ambient imagery can also be deliberately nonliteral.

**Investigate when.** A mock terminal, rising chart, notification, or calendar implies functionality or measured results that do not exist. A fake control appears interactive, and a demo state is presented as customer evidence.

**Review test.** Trace each fragment to a real capability or an explicitly labeled illustrative purpose. Ask what a viewer would reasonably infer. For actual applications, test whether controls work and data has an intelligible meaning.

**Recommended response.** Use a focused demonstration of a supported task. Label synthetic data or concept imagery where needed. Remove false affordances and unsupported metrics; preserve intentional nonliteral decoration that is not misleading.

**Protect this counterexample.** A simplified illustration is not dishonest merely because it is not a literal screenshot. The important question is the claim and expectation it creates.

**Research basis:** [S17](sources.md#s17), [S29](sources.md#s29), [S38](sources.md#s38).

## P08 — Contrast constructions

**Dimension:** rhetoric

Formulaic contrast substitutes for an informative explanation of the offer.

**Legitimate use.** Contrast can correct a real misconception, distinguish a product category, or express a deliberate rhetorical voice.

**Investigate when.** The sentence rejects vague alternatives, replaces one unsupported superlative with another, or keeps the reader from learning the concrete capability and limitation.

**Review test.** Identify the misconception being corrected. Ask whether the statement leaves a reader better able to predict what happens next. Check capability claims independently of stylistic rhythm.

**Recommended response.** Lead with the actual benefit, mechanism, or boundary. Keep contrast when it resolves genuine confusion; edit empty performance language rather than banning sentence forms.

**Protect this counterexample.** A phrase structure is not evidence of authorship. A clear contrast can be both accurate and memorable.

**Research basis:** [S20](sources.md#s20), [S29](sources.md#s29).

## P09 — Category counts

**Dimension:** classification and completeness

The number of categories is driven by the grid rather than by the subject.

**Legitimate use.** Three real tiers, peer benefits, findings, or alternatives can be exactly the right amount. A deliberate rhetorical triad can also be appropriate for the medium.

**Investigate when.** Important items are merged, omitted, or invented so that every section has the same item count. False equivalence turns unequal options into apparent peers.

**Review test.** Compare the content inventory with the displayed categories. Check whether omitted details matter to the decision. Separate the count of meaningful categories from their arrangement across rows.

**Recommended response.** Preserve the actual inventory and meaningful distinctions. Change layout to accommodate content; prioritize or progressively disclose when volume is genuinely too high.

**Protect this counterexample.** The existence of three items is not a defect. A fourth fabricated item is worse than a justified triad.

**Research basis:** [S11](sources.md#s11), [S18](sources.md#s18).

## P10 — Parallel wording

**Dimension:** content structure

Visual regularity is being purchased by flattening differences in meaning or content length.

**Legitimate use.** Parallel syntax and repeated fields can make peer items easier to scan. Similar actions should have predictably identifiable labels.

**Investigate when.** Copy is padded, facts are dropped, or translated text is clipped to match a template. Distinct concepts are turned into interchangeable benefit sentences.

**Review test.** Read the content without its containers. Look for missing distinctions and filler. Test naturally short and long labels, translated strings, user-generated content, and absent optional fields.

**Recommended response.** Keep stable fields and parallel labels where they improve comprehension. Let content height and explanatory detail vary when the information requires it. Repair containers rather than silently shortening substantive text.

**Protect this counterexample.** Equal-height components and consistent grammar are not intrinsically false. They are useful when the content is genuinely comparable and remains complete.

**Research basis:** [S07](sources.md#s07), [S10](sources.md#s10).

## P11 — Verifiable attribution

**Dimension:** evidence and trust

Authority language creates confidence without enough traceable evidence to evaluate the claim.

**Legitimate use.** A concise attributed finding can support a decision when a reader can access its source and understand the relevant scope. A qualitative source can be appropriate; not every claim needs a percentage.

**Investigate when.** Anonymous experts, invented studies, fabricated testimonials, or unexplained numbers are displayed as evidence. A true citation is attached to a claim it does not actually support.

**Review test.** Open the original source; verify existence, the exact claim, study context, and date. Distinguish measurement, interpretation, opinion, projection, and placeholder. Ask whether a reasonable reader would be misled.

**Recommended response.** Attribute and scope evidence that exists. Remove, qualify, or mark unverified claims when evidence is absent. Do not search for an impressive-looking source merely to decorate a conclusion already chosen.

**Protect this counterexample.** A citation marker alone does not make a claim sound. Conversely, an attributed quotation or supported broad explanation does not need to be rewritten into a statistical claim.

**Research basis:** [S29](sources.md#s29).

## P12 — Situated voice

**Dimension:** voice and cultural context

Informality is added as a generic marker of being human instead of reflecting a situated voice.

**Legitimate use.** A community’s own language, a creator’s recognizable voice, or a brand’s established colloquial register can be appropriate. Neutral corporate English is not the universal standard of authenticity.

**Investigate when.** Slang contradicts the emotional situation, is not understood by the audience, imitates a community as a stereotype, or erases a person’s own phrasing in favor of generic internet banter.

**Review test.** Identify who is speaking, to whom, in what situation, and on whose authority. Compare with approved voice examples. Involve relevant community or locale reviewers rather than treating demographic categories as voice presets.

**Recommended response.** Use direct language when context is missing; preserve intentional local expression when supported. Adjust the tone to the situation without changing the speaker’s identity. Flag cultural fit as a review question when evidence is absent.

**Protect this counterexample.** Do not label dialects or slang inherently unprofessional. The India/US research in this pack is not direct evidence about every vernacular or every community.

**Research basis:** [S19](sources.md#s19), [S23](sources.md#s23).

## P13 — Repeated composition

**Dimension:** composition and information architecture

Only the visual skin changes while every communication task inherits the same structure.

**Legitimate use.** A shared shell, repeated navigation, brand tokens, or consistent slide family can create cohesion. Repeated structures are especially useful for comparable cases or repeated measurements.

**Investigate when.** Comparison, explanation, workflow, and evidence are all squeezed into one hero-plus-feature grid. The system offers “alternatives” that differ only in color while preserving the actual mismatch.

**Review test.** Classify the communication or interaction task before comparing layouts. Identify which elements should remain invariant and which relationships need a different representation. Inspect the whole sequence or task flow, not isolated screenshots.

**Recommended response.** Preserve the visual system and familiar controls while changing composition when meaning changes: an aligned comparison, a worked example, a process diagram, a source-backed evidence view, or a focused action screen.

**Protect this counterexample.** A new layout on every slide can create needless relearning. Structural diversity is a capability to deploy when warranted, not a mandatory diversity quota.

**Research basis:** [S06](sources.md#s06), [S25](sources.md#s25), [S27](sources.md#s27).

## Boundaries worth preserving

**P04 versus P09:** P04 concerns the sequence and dependencies of a task. P09 concerns the completeness and truth of categories. A truthful five-category page might still use a three-column responsive grid.

**P10 versus P13:** P10 concerns parallel wording and component-level structure. P13 concerns the composition of a page, screen, or slide. A presentation can retain consistent typography and sentence conventions while using different evidence structures.

**P11 is not merely aesthetic:** Unsupported evidence can mislead even when the typography is excellent. Treat its consequences separately from a preference about serif type or card borders.
