# Decision patterns and receipts

## Choose the rule
| Situation | Useful process | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| One authorized decision maker | Gather evidence/options; owner decides with rationale | Do not label consultation as consensus |
| Group delegated authority | Agree a decision rule before evaluating options | Attendance alone does not define authority |
| Prioritizing further exploration | Preference vote followed by evidence/feasibility check | Votes do not validate demand |
| Multiple explicit criteria | Comparison table; optional weighted scoring | Weight choices and estimates drive the result |
| Important unknown | Small test or conditional decision | Do not vote uncertainty away |
| Discovery-only request | Preserve options, evidence, and questions | No premature convergence |

## Criteria
Define each criterion, unit or anchor, direction, evidence, and uncertainty. For “impact,” name who benefits, what changes, and over what horizon. For “effort,” define the work and dependencies. Unknown is distinct from high effort or low value.

If using weighted scores, show the weight rationale and test whether a reasonable change in weights alters the ranking. Do not use precise decimal scores when evidence is categorical. Record the judgment honestly.

## Dissent and ties
Invite concerns that could invalidate the leading option. Distinguish preference, constraint, factual objection, and value disagreement. For a tie use the agreed rule or decision owner; do not invent authority after the vote. A provisional experiment can resolve uncertainty if the group has authority to run it.

## Decision receipt
Decision or recommendation; date/context; owner/authority; options considered; criteria/rule; evidence; selected option; rationale; dissent; assumptions; next action; proposed/accepted owner; deadline; revisit trigger.

Example: “Recommend testing A with three representative tasks. Final product choice remains with the sponsor. Two concerns about access remain unresolved. Owner acceptance pending.”
That is a useful output without falsely claiming consensus or commitment.

## Options to accepted action on a shared board

1. Establish the question, scope, authority, constraints, common criteria, and tie procedure before evaluating options. A named owner is one possible rule; use the group's actual rule. No authority means a recommendation awaiting a named approval step.
2. Invite independent thinking before showing collective choices. Give every candidate a stable ID and similar visual treatment. Merge genuine duplicates only with contributor agreement or clear acknowledgement; retain original text and all source IDs. A broad theme and a concrete intervention are not comparable candidate units.
3. Shortlist only when needed. With a few viable options, compare directly. A preference vote is input, not approval. Record eligibility reasons, boundary ties, and why a material low-vote option is retained. Keep the remaining candidates findable. Explain actual attribution limits and treat vote-distribution rules as facilitation rules unless technically verified; do not infer anonymity from hidden choices or automatic ending from a timer.
4. Show the shortlisted alternatives together against the same criteria, with concise judgments, reasons, evidence links, and explicit Unknowns. Include a current-state baseline only when meaningful. Do not manufacture RICE inputs or scores; a computed rank does not settle dependencies or authority.
5. Record selected candidate IDs or explicit defer/no-action, rationale, alternatives not chosen, material concerns and responses, key uncertainty, owner/rule, confirmation state, and revisit trigger. Silence is not consensus. Insufficient evidence can justify a small test or deferral.
6. Link each action to the decision. Capture accepting owner or `unconfirmed`, first step, due date when supplied, success signal, review point, dependency, and status. Prefer a few resourced commitments; one to three is a starting budget, not a rule that discards necessary work. An absent person's name does not establish acceptance. Missing dates remain inputs.

The durable trace is evidence/need ID → candidate ID → decision ID → action ID. Use native labeled fields or an existing record format, not a new application. Keep concise decision context visible during action planning. A reviewer should be able to reconstruct why the choice was made and who actually accepted what.

Source and limits: [collaborative-board handoff](../../workshop-design/references/facilitation-evidence.md#collaborative-board-handoff). This process is a proposed adaptation, not a tested claim that a particular board or voting method improves decisions.
