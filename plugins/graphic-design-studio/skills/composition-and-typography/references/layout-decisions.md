# Layout decisions

These are practical diagnostic moves, not universal aesthetic rules. Supporting source distinctions are in [research](../../graphic-design/references/research.md).

| Symptom | Inspect | First useful move | Confirm in render |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Everything competes | Relative area, contrast, weight, faces, repeated badges | Demote one competing emphasis; enlarge or isolate the intended lead | First glance lands in the intended place |
| Empty but unbalanced | Mass distribution and transparent image padding | Shift a group or change its crop; judge visible silhouettes | The void feels intentional rather than accidental |
| Static lineup | Equal heights, evenly spaced heads, parallel baselines | Vary group scale or overlap around one anchor | An intentional rhythm; all required subjects still legible |
| Dynamic but chaotic | Competing axes, repeated effects, broken alignments | Keep one dominant gesture and restore a calm information block | Energy serves the message rather than obscuring it |
| Text feels pasted on | Image texture, color values, margins, type-image relationship | Reposition into real negative space or introduce a local reading field | Important letters remain distinguishable across their whole area |
| Headline feels awkward | Word shape, counters, kerning, line breaks | Rebreak by meaning; adjust optical spacing without stretching glyphs | No accidental joins or isolated fragments |
| Bottom feels cramped | Number of facts, QR footprint, edge allowance | Remove duplicate explanation and regroup facts before resizing type | Action and logistical details can be read and found |
| Style feels generic | Specificity to this event and actual reference | Strengthen the concept through subject, crop, type, or message | The result has a reason for its visual choices |

## Selecting a structure

Use a dominant-image layout when atmosphere or recognition is the main attraction and the supplied image can carry it. Reserve sufficient space for meaningful facts before enlarging art. Avoid covering identity-bearing details just to fill a rectangle.

Use a typographic layout when the name, statement, or offer is itself the image. This is appropriate without decorative illustration. Distinguish roles through size, weight, line breaks, and space; more fonts are rarely the first remedy.

Use an asymmetrical editorial layout when a strong text block and image can balance across unequal areas. Define shared alignment points; otherwise two separate layouts can coexist on one sheet without relating.

Use a field-and-band layout when artwork is rich and logistics are substantial. The information band can be part of the concept rather than a generic footer pasted under it. Test how much of the sheet the band consumes at the intended viewing distance.

Use modular structure when a series needs reusable positions or multiple offers must be compared. Equal modules are justified by equal content roles, not by a template's defaults. One visual interruption can signal priority while the rest maintains rhythm.

## Optical checks

Look at subject silhouettes, not PNG dimensions. Inspect a cutout's alpha bounds before determining its apparent size. Character height and narrative prominence are separate choices: an intentionally small character can remain small while gaining visibility through spacing and contrast.

Watch for almost-touching edges: a weapon ending exactly at a letter, a head tangent to a border, or feet resting accidentally on a rule. Either separate them clearly or overlap them convincingly. Do not use collision avoidance so aggressively that every object becomes isolated.

Compare a blur or squint view for broad value masses, a full view for the reading sequence, and close-ups for optical refinements. A computed center of mass can help explain an arrangement; it cannot replace visual judgment.

## Type and color checks

Large display letters may need manual pair adjustments. Uppercase labels can benefit from spacing; a script face can be damaged by it. Tracking is a type-specific choice, not a percentage to apply globally. Read numerals in dates and prices, punctuation, long names, accented letters, and symbols in the actual font.

Do not infer physical legibility from point size alone. Size labels describe font metrics; different faces have different visible proportions. Check at final dimensions and expected distance. Keep printer constraints separate from a screen screenshot's apparent scale.

For a restrained palette, assign a background field, primary ink, and accent role. More colors are appropriate when they organize a genuine system or match the visual concept. Check grayscale as a diagnostic for hue-dependent meaning, not as a requirement that every color poster work identically in monochrome.

For digital text, contrast calculations apply to the actual foreground and background. A sample from a quiet corner of a photograph cannot stand in for the whole word area. A contrast pass also cannot certify reading comfort, thin strokes, textured print stock, glare, or viewing distance.

For a repeated series or theme change, check each used text/surface and meaningful-symbol/surface pair, including muted copy and tinted panels. Keep accent, caution and risk roles intelligible; verify dark variants independently. If the same pairing fails across several graphics, correct the existing palette or shared template and recheck representative outputs instead of repairing each export in isolation.
