# Composition and typography

Start from the message and visual source. Inspect the actual artwork, not just bounding boxes or CSS. For reviews, identify the relevant anchors before describing a visual problem.

Establish a dominant element and a readable route to the decision and action. Map large masses before details. Build a few shared alignments, then judge their optical result: transparent padding, a face, dark clothing, or a large letter can shift apparent balance away from geometric centers.

Balance does not require matching sides. Counter a large quiet shape with a smaller high-contrast area, shift the focal group, or use empty space deliberately. If the composition feels static, change scale relationships, grouping, crop, or direction before adding texture. When a direction is already approved, adjust the smallest part that solves the issue.

Create energy through a coherent gesture: a stepped headline, diagonal sight line, asymmetrical mass, purposeful overlap, or interruption of a repeated rhythm. Keep a stable area for essential information. Avoid random rotation of every object. Check that gestures, gaze, and props guide attention usefully and that near-touches do not create accidental tangencies.

Assign type by role: expressive display, readable information, action, and supporting detail. One family with useful weights may suffice; two contrasting families are a practical starting point, not a quota. Evaluate the actual words, numerals, language support, and optical size. Avoid horizontal distortion and automatic shrinking to force a fit. Tune line breaks, leading, kerning, and tracking in the rendered composition.

Give color a job: structure, emphasis, identity, atmosphere, or differentiation. Check value contrast before relying on hue. Do not apply universal color-emotion claims. Preserve a supplied palette unless the requested change or a legibility problem warrants revision.

For article images and thumbnails, avoid treating text reduction or a neutral palette as the whole redesign. First make the visual relationship legible: what changed, what is being compared, where the claim connects to its source, or what the viewer should notice. Change the large masses and focal relationship before decorating a weak layout. Choose a vivid accent, strong light/dark separation or expressive crop when it supports the subject; restraint is control of emphasis, not a requirement for muted beige cards. Keep secondary explanation in the article when the image can carry the core idea more directly.

Inspect at thumbnail scale, intended viewing size, and detail scale. Thumbnail review tests dominant masses; it cannot prove body readability. Apply digital contrast criteria where appropriate, and test printed materials in their actual conditions when available.

Read [the decision guide](references/layout-decisions.md) for diagnosing a specific problem. For final review use [graphic-design-review](../graphic-design-review/SKILL.md). Explain decisions as “change → perceptual effect → reader benefit,” not a list of design terms.

For a new direction, alternative routes on one brief, or recurring generic layouts, use [reader-task art direction](references/art-direction-brief-selector.md) and read the relevant complete direction cards. Vary route, audience agency, grouping or information structure within approved identity; a small approved-artwork repair does not need this pass.
