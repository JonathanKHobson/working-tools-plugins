# Lesson Experience Design

Build the requested lesson, not merely an outline. Infer a reasonable audience and duration when missing and state assumptions briefly. If a lesson is synchronous, include facilitator language and timing; if asynchronous, include navigation, checks, and recovery instructions.

## Sequence by learner need

Apply [learning beyond the activity](../instructional-design/references/addie-decisions.md#preserve-learning-beyond-the-activity) to explanations, examples, practice, feedback and the recap; make the idea and its later use explicit inside the activity.
Begin with relevance and a small diagnostic task. Teach just enough for the next meaningful action. Show a worked example with concise visible rationale, offer a supported attempt, then reduce support for an independent attempt. Do not demand productive struggle before a novice has enough knowledge to attempt the task.

Choose one likely misconception and design an example/non-example comparison that exposes it. Prompt learners to explain a decision, predict an outcome, or retrieve a key idea before checking a model answer. Feedback should name the observable discrepancy and support another attempt.

End with a changed-context task: same underlying principle, different surface details. Avoid labeling a copied demonstration as independent transfer. Adapt pacing to pilot observations rather than a supposed universal attention-span number.

## Deliver
Include learner-facing directions, materials, completed example, practice task, answer/rubric guidance, feedback and retry path, approximate timing, and one later-use prompt. Keep facilitator notes separate from learner content. For young learners specify developmentally suitable reading, supervision, and participation supports; do not assume adult workplace norms.

Use the relevant [lesson patterns](references/lesson-patterns.md) section for examples, coaching, online discussion, or game mechanics. Use [learning science](../instructional-design/references/learning-science.md) only when the method needs an evidence rationale. A session with a group decision as its primary output belongs in workshop design; a session intended to build individual capability belongs here.

For a lesson taught through a shared board or staged canvas, use the [shared-board lesson pattern](references/lesson-patterns.md#shared-board-lesson-pattern) to keep the current learner task, its inputs, completion cue, and next use clear without hiding needed evidence or comparisons.

For an engaging diagnostic, retrieval, reasoning, revision or transfer activity, search the [activity archive](references/activity-library.md) and inspect only a few matching cards. Each adaptation must produce observable evidence and a feedback/retry move. Keep individual evidence separate from team success, confidence, enjoyment and performance style. Prefer an untimed, noncompetitive equivalent when speed or public performance is incidental. Draft File-O-Fun cards and Atlas notes are inspiration with provenance; they are not proven assessment methods.

For an external programming tutorial, exercise sequence or project-learning resource, use [technical resource selection](references/technical-learning-resource-selection.md) to check the actual destination, prerequisites, version, access, rights and setup before assigning one bounded task.
