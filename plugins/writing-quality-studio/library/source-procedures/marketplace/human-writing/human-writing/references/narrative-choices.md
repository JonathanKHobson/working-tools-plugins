# Narrative choices

New first-party support guide, authored for Writing Quality Studio on 2026-10-10. This implements the recovered Human Writing method's narrative-audit requirements. It is a current repair, not a recovered historical reference or a reconstruction of a research paper. Copyright 2026 Jonathan Kyle Hobson. CC BY-SA 4.0.

## Establish the story contract

Use this guide for prose with characters and events: fiction, memoir, anecdotes or a narrative case study. An email, product description or reference explanation does not need a narrative audit merely because it needs clearer prose.

Identify the story's purpose, intended reader, genre, scope, authorized invention, continuity constraints, perspective and desired degree of revision. Separate established events and quotations from imagined material. Fiction can develop details within the brief; memoir and case studies cannot acquire plausible memories, dialogue, motives or outcomes without support.

Create a small working inventory of the relevant characters, desires, choices, events, consequences, time relationships and unresolved questions. Preserve ambiguity when it is part of the story. Do not assume every story needs a conflict, a twist, a moral or a tidy resolution.

## Audit choices before sentences

| Choice | Questions for this story | Possible repair and reason to keep |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Character agency | Who wants what, who chooses, and what changes because of that choice? | Clarify a supported decision or consequence. Keep passivity when helplessness or observation is deliberate; do not assign invented motives. |
| Causality | Can the reader distinguish a cause, a sequence and a coincidence? | Repair a missing connection established by the source. Keep an unexplained event when uncertainty is intentional. |
| Theme | Does meaning emerge from events, or does narration repeatedly announce it? | Remove a redundant explanation or give a supported consequence room. Keep direct reflection when the narrator or audience needs it. |
| Time | What is the event order, and when does the reader learn each event? | Clarify an accidental temporal jump. Keep a purposeful delay, recollection or non-linear sequence. |
| Revelation | What does the reader know, expect and discover at each point? | Move information when its current placement weakens comprehension or intended tension. Keep restraint without withholding a fact needed to follow the scene. |
| Perspective | Whose experience is available, and how close is the narration? | Repair an unintended viewpoint slip. Keep deliberate changes when their signals and purpose are clear. |
| Setting | Which supplied or authorized details affect action, perception or stakes? | Use a detail that matters. Do not decorate nonfiction with invented sensory experience; keep spare settings when appropriate. |
| Reader relationship | Is the narrator inviting, withholding, confessing, observing or explaining? | Align direct address and explanation with that relationship. Keep distance, discomfort or disagreement when intended. |
| Resolution | Does the ending follow the story's consequences and remaining uncertainty? | Remove a convenient unsupported resolution. Keep closure or an open ending when earned and requested. |

These are choices to consider, not a quota of features to add. A simple linear story may work. A character can have one clear goal. An explicit theme can suit a teaching story. An ending may resolve the main problem. The test is whether the current choice serves this piece and preserves its contract.

## Make the structural revision

1. Read the whole passage and state the effect it is meant to have. Mark the established event sequence, viewpoint and content that must remain.
2. Identify at most a few structural problems that interfere with that effect. Name their locations and explain the reader consequence.
3. Choose the smallest useful revision: reorder an event, clarify a choice, adjust revelation, remove repeated interpretation, restore perspective or strengthen an already authorized consequence.
4. For fiction, make any new event or detail consistent with the user's world and permission to invent. For nonfiction, use only supported material and label inference where appropriate.
5. Check the revised structure before polishing. Every new causal link, motive, remembered line or conclusion needs the correct authority.
6. Apply [the prose-pattern pass](prose-patterns.md) to sentences and rhythm without erasing the chosen perspective, purposeful ambiguity or emotional register.
7. Compare the result with the event inventory and desired effect. Verify chronology, causality, agency, perspective, continuity, quotations and what remains unresolved.

For review mode, return findings and proposed edits. For a requested rewrite, return the revised passage first. Do not generate alternative structures unless comparison is useful for the task or the user requests them.

## Synthetic fictional example

**Brief:** Revise this small fictional scene. Keep Mara at a shut ticket counter, the canceled last bus, her unanswered call to her brother, her decision to wait under the station clock and the unresolved journey. Close third-person viewpoint. No rescue or new character is authorized.

**Draft:** “Mara arrived at the station. It was a place of journeys and possibilities. The last bus had been canceled. Her brother did not answer her call. She realized that life was unpredictable, but hope would always guide her. She decided to wait under the clock. In the end, everything would work out.”

**Revision:** “The ticket counter was shut. Mara read the cancellation notice for the last bus, then called her brother. No answer. She went back to the station clock and waited beneath it.”

The revision organizes the scene around what Mara encounters and does. It preserves the cancellation, unanswered call, waiting choice, setting and unresolved journey. It removes a repeated theme and an unsupported happy ending. It does not invent her brother's intention, a childhood memory, weather, a rescue or a second viewpoint.

**Retain:** A supplied ending in which Mara explains why she chose to wait can remain if reflection is part of her voice. Do not remove every explicit thought because this example uses external action. If the brief instead asks for a fable with a stated moral, that requested form governs.

## Source and scope

[Human Writing](../SKILL.md) names character agency, causality, theme, time, revelation, perspective, setting and reader relationship as narrative concerns and limits its StoryScope discussion to long-form fiction. This guide implements those named concerns as original editorial questions and synthetic examples. It does not assert findings, effects, frequencies or recommendations from the linked paper beyond the original method's stated scope. Consult the actual source if a task asks for research claims; do not treat this repair as a paper summary.

The source inventory and final meaning check use Writing Quality Studio's maintained [editorial gate](../../../../../../skills/natural-prose-editor/references/editorial-gate.md). Evidence verification and authorial judgment remain distinct from an editorial exercise. No human preference or narrative-quality effect has been measured for this guide.
