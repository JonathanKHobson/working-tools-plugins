# Language-sensitive editing

Use when the request is to translate or localize prose, improve English written by a learner, or revise for a multilingual audience. A dialect or non-native construction is not automatically an error. Establish target language, region, reader and register from the request; ask only when a missing choice would materially change meaning or forms of address. A supplied author sample or publication convention outranks generic assumptions about “native” style.

## Translation and localization

Translate the message and its level of formality, not each word in isolation. Preserve names, numbers, quotations, links, commands, technical terms and claim strength. Check address forms, date and number conventions, units, cultural references and idioms for the chosen region. Keep an official name or required term in its official form; explain it if necessary rather than silently translating it. If a phrase has two plausible interpretations, flag the ambiguity instead of choosing a new promise. Do not claim native fluency or publication readiness from a machine-only pass.

Example: a tentative English “We might be able to start in October” must remain tentative in the target language; the warmer register must not turn it into a confirmed start date.

## Edit learner English without erasing the writer

Give the requested clean version first. Preserve the writer's factual content, level of confidence, directness and personal voice. Correct grammar, articles, tense and collocations where they obstruct or distract, but do not replace every simple sentence with a more formal one. If the user wants to learn, show only the recurring patterns that would help their next draft, with a short original → revision → reason example. Distinguish a grammar correction from an optional style choice. Do not infer skill, identity or origin from the draft.

For broad audiences or translation preparation, prefer explicit actors, clear referents, unambiguous dates and units, and control names rather than color or position alone. Check that idiom removal did not flatten a deliberate voice choice. For technical procedures, also use [technical clarity](technical-clarity.md); preserve literal commands and the difference between must, can and might.


## Cultural voice and uncertainty

Treat authenticity as faithfulness to a named referent: the supplied author samples, requested community/publication convention, or a voice owner's stated intention. Do not assign slang, warmth, directness, idioms or “Western professionalism” from nationality, age, ethnicity or another demographic category. Preserve deliberate regional expression unless the requested audience or register gives a specific reason to change it. A requested slang style is a task voice, not proof of membership in a community.

When culturally meaningful phrasing is unclear, retain it or offer a labeled alternative and identify the uncertainty; seek a qualified reader or voice owner when the consequence warrants it. Do not invent a cultural rationale to justify a rewrite. A learner's language choices are not evidence of their identity or ability. The [plain-language adapter's evidence note](../../plain-language-editing/references/contextual-plain-language.md#evidence-and-limits) records the limited research basis for this safeguard; it does not prescribe demographic voice profiles.

**Revise:** A formal community notice becomes “Hey besties, this is a total game changer!” solely because a brief says the audience is young. Restore the supplied register and concrete message unless the voice owner requested that language.

**Retain:** An authorized speaker sample uses “y'all” naturally in an invitation. Keep it when it fits that speaker and audience; do not replace it with “all stakeholders” merely to impose a corporate register.
