# Communication practice and reader review

Read when the user asks to learn a communication move, rehearse it, simulate a reader, or review a message whose stakes justify a deliberate audit. Keep the requested output first. A routine draft needs neither a lesson nor a scored rubric.

Choose the mode from the request: **create** a draft, **critique** an existing one, **coach** a choice, **practice** under a constraint, **simulate** a plausible reader reaction, or **teach** a pattern with a worked example. If coaching, identify what the reader should understand or do, the proof available, the real uncertainty, and the success criterion. For sensitive or strategic work, consider speaker, audience, relationship, channel, stakes, power, likely misread, and the action sought; use only the fields that change the draft.

For practice, show one compact worked example and why it works, then give a partial scaffold and a fresh attempt under the user's actual constraints. Review that attempt against audience fit, clarity, tone, evidence, ask, and risk. Finish with one transfer variation, such as a skeptical or rushed reader, written versus spoken delivery, or a change in authority. Do not infer personal traits or turn the exercise into a fixed personality simulation. Use the [practice-loop template](../templates/practice-loop.md) when a reusable worksheet helps.

For a high-stakes audit, name the strongest current passage and the first revision that would change the reader's decision. Check intent, audience fit, hook payoff, structure, proof, voice, brevity, tone, ethical pressure, and the proportionality of the ask. If the user requests scoring, rate only relevant dimensions on a 1–5 scale and explain the evidence behind each rating; do not score by default. Use credibility, reasoning, emotional relevance, and timing as lenses when one is missing or overplayed. For a tense message, check defensiveness, overexplaining, blame, pressure, desperation, coldness, and whether dignity and the relationship survive the revision. A reader simulation is a hypothesis: what would a recruiter, client, stakeholder, or skeptical reader understand, trust, question, and do next? Do not report an imagined reaction as observed user research.

When pacing or agency is the problem, use the [reader-loop audit](../templates/reader-loop-audit.md): arrival, reader goal, question or tension, proof and feedback, choice, and takeaway. Ask what promise the reader sees, what primary action they can take, and what feedback shows why the claim matters. Put the useful fact early; reveal order must improve comprehension, not hide basics. Show real consequences for yes, no, or wait when a decision is needed. Avoid unsupported urgency, gimmicks, and interaction that delays the reader's task. For a dense message, remove extraneous context, chunk necessary complexity, and move optional detail after the main point.
