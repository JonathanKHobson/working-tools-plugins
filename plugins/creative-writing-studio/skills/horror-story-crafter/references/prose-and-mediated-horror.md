# Prose and Mediated Horror

Use this reference for novels, short stories, scripts, audio drama, found footage, analog horror, screenlife, epistolary fiction, and dossier narratives.

## Long and short prose

For a novel or novella, build a causal spine rather than a sequence of set pieces:

1. establish the protectable life and its existing fracture;
2. make the first disturbance interpretable in more than one dangerous way;
3. let investigation or resistance expose a repeatable pattern;
4. make new knowledge increase responsibility or cost;
5. narrow apparent solutions while preserving real choices;
6. force a climax that acts on the story's central value conflict;
7. resolve immediate causality while preserving only the ambiguity that creates the intended aftertaste.

Subplots should change access, belief, obligation, or cost in the main horror engine. If removing a subplot changes nothing about the protagonist's decisions, threat knowledge, or attachments, compress or cut it.

For a short story, enter near the first meaningful disturbance, limit the number of rules and relationships, and make each image perform more than one job. Compression should intensify causality, not merely omit explanation.

## Viewpoint and prose control

- Select sensory details the viewpoint would notice for a reason: profession, fear, desire, habit, injury, or relationship.
- Vary psychic distance deliberately. Close distance intensifies bodily or interpretive pressure; wider distance can create fatalism, social scale, or procedural chill.
- Let syntax and paragraphing track attention. Shortness is not automatically tension, and fragments lose force when constant.
- Use metaphor from the narrator's world rather than a generic horror vocabulary.
- Earn interior labels such as fear or grief with behavior, memory, judgment, or consequence when those add meaning.

## Openings and chapter turns

An opening should establish a normal grammar and create a question with stakes. It need not begin with violence. Useful openings combine an ordinary objective, a specific relationship or obligation, and one detail that does not fit safely.

End a scene or chapter when a choice, fact, arrival, absence, or cost changes the next objective. Avoid ending every unit with an unexplained noise or artificial cliffhanger.

## Anticipation, pressure, and complicity

The frightening image is not the same as fear. Give the audience time to anticipate what the image could cost. Establish a pattern through traces, interrupted routines, aftermath, glimpses, or consequences before a full encounter when delay strengthens investment and inference.

Combine pressures selectively:

- **Psychological pressure:** denial, obsession, guilt, duty, self-justification, or a theory the viewpoint needs to remain true.
- **Physical pressure:** shrinking routes, time, privacy, tools, help, or safe procedures.
- **Narrative pressure:** viable choices protect different values, and inaction is also a choice with a legible cost.

Pressure should narrow comfort, not erase agency. Let the character make understandable bad choices from partial evidence, obligation, temptation, or prior compromise. Complicity is strongest when the audience can follow the reasoning one step past the point where they would like to stop.

Ask: **If the monster or supernatural explanation vanished, what conflict would still hurt?** Keep that human pressure active before, during, and after the reveal.

For paranoia, obsession, unreliable experience, memory or identity erosion, dangerous knowledge, desire traps, or a sustained internal pressure arc, also read [psychological horror and perception](psychological-horror-and-perception.md).

## Atmosphere as information control

Atmosphere is the pattern of attention: what the viewpoint notices, what is withheld, when a detail recurs, and how its meaning changes. Select a small number of sensory or material anchors and let later evidence revise them. A repeated sound can move from background nuisance to signal, measurement, lure, or proof.

Do not inventory every sense in every scene. Choose details that orient, implicate the viewpoint, teach a rule, contradict a model, or change a choice. Ambience supports causality; it cannot replace it.

## Material setting

When a place carries the horror, let it behave consistently across scenes. Establish material facts the viewpoint can test: what marks, shifts, sticks, echoes, stains, opens, refuses, or remembers. Repeat selected features only when their meaning or state has changed.

A location description is strongest when it does at least two jobs: orient the reader, reveal character attention, teach a threat rule, foreshadow a cost, constrain action, or show residue from an earlier choice. Avoid adjective stacks that announce horror without supplying new evidence. Do not tell the reader that a place is terrifying when its observable behavior can earn that judgment.

## Found footage, analog, screenlife, and documents

The medium must participate in causality. Define:

- why the record exists;
- who operates, edits, stores, or transmits it;
- its intended audience;
- technological and institutional constraints;
- why recording or communicating continues under pressure;
- which gaps are natural consequences of the medium;
- what metadata, framing, compression, permissions, or interface behavior reveals;
- how the final audience plausibly receives it.

Treat glitches as evidence with rules, not supernatural confetti. A missing minute, corrupted frame, automated caption, repeated notification, camera boundary, or moderation action should alter interpretation or choice.

Let an artifact preserve a changing self as well as information. A journal, specimen book, archive, recording, photograph, collection, or annotated object records the maker's earlier intention; later entries, handling, omissions, or reinterpretations reveal what that person can now recognize. Revisit it only when its role changes—for example from play to proof, possession to guilt, protection to leverage, or shame to inheritance.

The artifact may preserve and harm at once. Make that contradiction material through custody, damage, arrangement, marginal notes, selection, or the repeated act used to create it. If a late disclosure reclassifies apparently severe harm as staged, symbolic, reversible, or misunderstood, establish evidence for that possibility and retain the earlier emotional and ethical consequences. Do not use a frame-note twist merely to cancel what made the record disturbing.

For a dossier or chain of records, identify each source's purpose, access, limitations, and interests. Independent records should corroborate a fact while complicating its meaning. Attribute contradictions to viewpoint, technology, translation, timing, custody, or incentive rather than treating inconsistency as automatic mystery. For evidence that destabilizes human models of reality, also read [cosmic horror and epistemic dread](cosmic-horror-and-epistemic-dread.md).

## Revision checks

- Can the central fear be named without naming the monster?
- Does each major scene change knowledge, obligation, access, relationship, or cost?
- Do clues recontextualize earlier evidence rather than invalidate it?
- Are descriptions doing action, interpretation, rhythm, or world work?
- Does the ending pay off earlier choices and leave deliberate residue?
- Does every return to an artifact change its meaning, custody, condition, or cost?
