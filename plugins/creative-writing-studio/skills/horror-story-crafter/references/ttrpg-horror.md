# TTRPG Horror

Use this reference for tabletop roleplaying one-shots, scenarios, campaigns, scenes, and GM-facing horror material.

## Prepare a situation, not a required plot

Define the threat, its current activity, what happens without intervention, and several actionable locations or people. Players should be able to choose approach, sequence, alliances, and costs. Prepare how the situation reacts rather than one mandatory chain of scenes.

Separate player consent from character control. Characters may be trapped, frightened, deceived, injured, or transformed while players retain boundaries, meaningful decisions, and a way to pause or redirect play.

Establish genre, intensity, and premise buy-in before play. Use character fears as fictional pressure; never surprise players by targeting a real phobia or disclosed vulnerability. A player may choose to let a character enter the basement, touch the relic, or accept the bargain because the danger is inviting, not because the GM removed every other verb.

## Scenario structure

Provide:

- an immediate invitation or problem with a reason to act now;
- a protectable person, place, relationship, identity, or future;
- a small set of nodes that can be approached in multiple orders; size the set to actual table time rather than requiring a minimum count;
- clues that connect nodes and change decisions;
- an escalating threat clock; add a separate offstage clock only when it creates a distinct playable consequence;
- threat rules the GM can apply consistently;
- consequences that alter the situation instead of stopping play;
- more than one viable end state shaped by player choices.

Do not gate a necessary clue behind one roll. Put core information on multiple paths, then let rolls determine speed, safety, detail, leverage, or cost. False models should be plausible interpretations of real evidence, not arbitrary misinformation.

### Collaborative outline and consultation

Keep established facts, reported play, candidate inventions, unresolved choices and exclusions visibly distinct. A repeated consultant suggestion is not user approval. Preserve disagreements between consultants instead of combining incompatible threat rules into one plot.

For each candidate, require one observable rule, independent evidence routes, a small successful test and at least two concrete finale interventions. Costs must follow the rule and player actions; a request for horror does not justify mandatory sacrifice or arbitrary loss. Unknown cosmic origins can coexist with reliable local cause and effect. Do not make private thoughts or a preferred interpretation the hidden victory condition.

Adapt workshop outlines as beat → player action → changed evidence or world → next available choice. Budget setup, breaks and debrief before allocating scenes. Keep optional nodes truly skippable. A recording earns its place when it changes an action, not merely when it delivers lore. Delegate sources, creative alternatives and diagrams separately, then reconcile them once against the current brief.

For a dangerous location, give the GM its sensory tell, trigger, response, counterplay, and residue. Observable facts do not require rolls. Use rolls when a character acts under pressure and both outcomes change play; do not stack escalating saves merely to force the table down the prepared route.

## Opening scene architecture

An opening should teach the table's tone and primary verbs while creating a visible direction for the next scene.

- Establish either a playable ordinary state or an explicit reason to be present before the first irreversible disturbance. If the horror depends on a tonal break, give the table enough ordinary social texture to feel its loss.
- When a sequel one-shot must also work for newcomers, establish the inherited victory before immersion: give a concise factual recap, then let each player author one visible residue or cost. Once the scene begins, let NPC and material reactions carry that history instead of pausing for retrospective questions.
- Put character- or setting-authorship questions before the immersive turn, or save them for a natural lull. Do not interrupt an escalating reveal to ask a question that could have been answered earlier.
- Give observable facts freely. Call for a roll only when a character acts under pressure and both success and failure would change the situation. Treat published lists of possible rolls as a response reservoir, not a mandatory script.
- Put one vulnerable person, disturbing fact, or physical problem within reach. Let the players' treatment of it change its behavior, the information it yields, or who becomes exposed.
- Reveal physical horror in interruptible pieces. Stop after each material change so action, silence, retreat, or intervention determines the next detail.
- Preserve causal mystery. Early evidence should support more than one honest interpretation while still pointing toward an actionable person, place, route, or threat.
- Name the scene's dramatic question in GM notes when it helps pacing, but do not announce it to players if that would break immersion.
- End on a concrete call to action with an honest cost for delay. If the players hesitate, state the cost rather than inventing a command or removing their choice.

### Escalate attention without forcing it

When a warning depends on a small environmental or animal response, present the first visible change, pause, and let player attention earn the next detail. If the table misses it, give the GM a short ladder of optional nudges and instruct them to choose only the smallest one needed. Useful nudges include a quiet sound growing audible, a simple magical effect visibly failing, or an NPC beginning an ordinary complaint and stopping when they recognize fear. Keep every image concrete enough to picture. Avoid unexplained fantasy shorthand that asks the GM to translate the sentence at the table.

An injured or shocked NPC can cling to a familiar procedure, phrase, or task. This creates uncanny behavior and directs attention toward a physical clue, but the GM-facing truth must state the NPC's origin, destination, objective, and misconception. Player-facing dialogue should make the immediate request understandable without revealing the larger mystery. Procedure is pressure and characterization, not an excuse for opaque jargon.

## GM-ready content

When a field requests content, write the actual usable content. Give exact names, clues, answers, sensory facts, NPC motives, clock advances, consequences, and speakable lines. Avoid filling an output with reminders such as "make this scary" or "tell players what they know."

Read-aloud should be short enough to interrupt, grounded in what characters can perceive, and free of decisions or emotions imposed on them. Put deeper context in GM notes.

For a named game system, use its mechanics accurately when known from supplied context or reliable local sources. If a mechanical choice materially depends on missing rules, remain system-neutral or ask one focused question rather than inventing rules.

## Agency under fear

Useful pressure includes:

- two protectable things that cannot both be secured;
- limited time with several defensible priorities;
- knowledge that creates responsibility;
- bargains with explicit benefits and costs;
- temporary safety that advances another clock;
- a threat that adapts visibly to repeated tactics.

Avoid predetermined capture, transformation, betrayal, or death unless the group explicitly accepted that premise and the players still control meaningful consequences around it.

## Delay the reveal, not player action

Let traces, consequences, partial views, recurring sounds, missing people, altered spaces, or threat behavior establish a pattern before the full confrontation. Players should be able to investigate, fortify, flee, bargain, rescue, or spend resources during that delay.

An encounter can begin before the threat is fully visible. Use initiative or another action-order procedure when timing and competing priorities matter, not only when a monster appears in attack range. Early actions should change position, evidence, exposure, routes, resources, or the state in which the threat arrives.

Keep sensory preparation short and usable. Choose one or two recurring anchors whose meaning changes. Ambience, lighting, props, and music may support attention, but the scenario must still create anticipation through information, consequence, and choice.

## Shift from dread to pursuit

Do not sustain helplessness for the entire scenario. Horror can begin with an unknown and apparently overwhelming threat, then move through a phase change:

1. players notice consequences and form competing models;
2. testing reveals partial rules, limits, and stakes;
3. the table chooses what it is willing to risk or protect;
4. players pursue, contain, bargain with, expose, redirect, escape, or exploit the threat on their own terms.

Partial knowledge should create agency without making the force routine. The table may still lose people, places, certainty, or safety, but their actions determine which loss occurs and what survives. A dark ending needs causal closure: show what the final choices saved, changed, transmitted, or made impossible.

## Let the first threat teach the scenario

The first substantial adversary is genre instruction. Its behavior should demonstrate what evidence matters, which player verbs work, what kind of violation the scenario permits, and what consequences can follow the party after the encounter.

A multi-form horror can create a useful sequence when each form changes the problem:

1. **Anomaly:** A small, fast, or incomplete form hides, observes, interrupts speech, or forces interaction with the room. Its job is uncertainty and pursuit, not heavy damage.
2. **Revelation:** The threat unfolds into its full body only after the table has spent time interpreting the smaller form. Its anatomy should explain its movement and signature attack rather than read like unrelated monster features.
3. **Residue:** Destruction releases fragments, contamination, witnesses, or evidence that changes the next scene. Cap cleanup with an escape, surrender, or containment trigger instead of fighting every remnant to the last HP.

Every phase change must alter available verbs, position, information, or stakes. A larger damage die alone is not a new phase. Preserve containment, escape, deception, or environmental problem-solving as viable answers when the fiction supports them.

Multi-form encounters consume real table time. Update the run clock when adding one, make the final phase short, and cut elsewhere rather than hiding the cost in an unchanged pacing estimate.

### Carry intervention across the reveal

When PCs can touch, restrain, heal, cut, warn, or strike before an adversary fully appears, their intervention must change the threat's starting state. Hand the choice into at least one concrete field: HP, Stress, exposure, position, blocked routes, target priority, available evidence, or who begins within reach. A different description with an unchanged encounter state is not a consequence.

Give the GM the exact roll only after the player declares an uncertain action. State the success edge and the Fear cost, then define what failure changes. Include no-roll retreat, refusal, or waiting when the outcome is already certain. Do not present the options to players as a combat menu.

## Safety calibration

Match safety tools to the table and intensity. Establish content boundaries, a pause or stop method, and any fade-to-black expectations before high-intensity material. Do not demand explanations for a boundary. After intense play, allow a brief debrief or tonal transition.

A general content warning is not blanket consent to every depiction. Calibrate categories separately from descriptive explicitness, especially for sexual violence, self-harm, torture, body horror, captivity, and mental-health themes. Preserve the intended pressure while changing the image, distance, or consequence when a boundary requires it.

Ask for boundaries without asking players to justify them. Keep the distinction between character vulnerability and player vulnerability explicit: the goal is not to produce real panic, disgust, humiliation, or loss of control at the table.

Treat table humor as a possible pressure release rather than automatic resistance to the game. Rebuild tension through consequence, changed information, sensory focus, or a meaningful choice.

## TTRPG deliverable

For a playable scenario, provide:

1. premise and table contract;
2. opening situation;
3. threat model and clocks;
4. node map with active NPCs;
5. redundant clue paths;
6. likely questions with actual answers;
7. consequences and fail-forward states;
8. climax choices and possible residues;
9. concise safety calibration.
