# Interactive Horror

Use this reference for video games, interactive fiction, branching narrative, and playable horror prototypes.

## Start with the player contract

Define the player's role and repeatable verbs before designing scares. The avatar may be frightened, injured, trapped, deceived, or transformed; the player still needs legible ways to observe, choose, test, risk, and adapt.

Constrained agency is stronger than arbitrary helplessness. Remove or corrupt a familiar verb only after teaching its normal value, and replace total loss of control with a new decision whenever possible.

## Build the play loop

Specify:

- **goal:** what the player is trying to protect, learn, reach, repair, or escape;
- **verbs:** what the player can do repeatedly;
- **threat loop:** how danger searches, signals, approaches, reacts, and recedes;
- **information:** what can be observed, tested, recorded, inferred, or misread;
- **resources:** what decisions each resource creates, not merely how scarce it is;
- **feedback:** how the game communicates state changes before consequences become arbitrary;
- **adaptation:** how the threat changes when the player relies on a strategy;
- **failure:** what persists after mistakes, capture, death, retreat, or restart.

Fear often comes from knowing what might work but not whether there is enough time, certainty, or acceptable cost.

## Make contact and communication playable

When the player must understand, bargain with, or care for an alien force, turn interpretation into action rather than lore selection.

- Define a contact medium and the operations the player can perform with it.
- Give each operation a learnable but incomplete effect on both sides.
- Separate comprehension, empathy, obedience, alliance, and forgiveness; success in one does not guarantee the others.
- Provide low-risk tests before irreversible messages, then let urgency tempt the player to communicate faster than they understand.
- Make miscommunication change behavior, relationship, space, or available verbs instead of merely producing a wrong-dialogue notification.
- Let both parties adapt. A force that learns the player's patterns can become easier to address and more capable of exploiting them.
- Consider one controlled viewpoint reversal that makes a familiar player action legible as threat, trespass, care, or injury from the other side.

The player may empathize and still refuse, contain, flee, or fight. Preserve the consequences of the force's actions even when its intent becomes sympathetic.

## Space and information

Spatial knowledge is a resource. Use landmarks, routes, locked relationships, sound paths, visibility, shortcuts, safe-room conditions, and environmental change to make navigation meaningful. Randomized spaces still need learnable local rules.

Place story information where play gives it meaning. A clue found through risk, sacrifice, mastery, or optional curiosity carries more weight than a lore dump disconnected from action.

For environmental hazards, design a learnable loop: **sensory tell → trigger → response → counterplay → residue**. The first contact may be uncertain, but attention or controlled testing should improve the player's model. A severe consequence needs stronger prior evidence than a reversible setback.

## Threat fairness

Telegraph danger proportionally to consequence. Feedback may be incomplete or deceptive, but it should become interpretable through attention and experience. Distinguish:

- what the threat can always do;
- what triggers escalation;
- what temporarily protects the player;
- what the threat learns or corrupts;
- how the player can recover, reroute, bargain, hide, confront, or accept a cost.

Unpredictability should emerge within rules. Pure randomness weakens dread because preparation and inference stop mattering.

## Failure and repetition

Choose what failure means: resource loss, changed world state, injured relationship, contamination, revealed information, altered route, narrative branch, or reset. Repeated death can create mastery, numbness, dread of accumulated cost, or meta-knowledge; design for the intended effect.

In a reset loop, decide what persists: knowledge, injury, relationship strain, contamination, opened routes, lost resources, environmental damage, or threat adaptation. Every return should add evidence or change a decision; stat erosion alone becomes grind.

Fail-forward consequences are useful when they create a worse or stranger situation rather than simply blocking progress. Do not make every mistake reversible if the fiction promises consequence.

## Interactive deliverable

For a game concept or scenario, provide:

1. player fantasy and protectable thing;
2. core verbs and loop;
3. threat rules, feedback, and adaptation;
4. information and spatial design;
5. resource decisions;
6. escalation states;
7. failure, recovery, and persistence;
8. climax choice and ending residue.

If communication is central, also specify the contact medium, translation feedback, mutual misunderstanding, escalation cost, and how each side changes through contact.
