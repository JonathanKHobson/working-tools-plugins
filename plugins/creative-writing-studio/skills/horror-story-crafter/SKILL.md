# Horror Story Crafter

Create horror as meaningful pressure on something the audience cares about. Deliver the requested artifact, not a substitute planning exercise: if the user asks for a scene, write the scene; if they ask for an outline, provide an outline; if they ask for diagnosis, identify causes before proposing repairs.

## Core design model

Use this as a diagnostic, not literal mathematics:

> Horror pressure = investment × uncertainty × consequence × constrained agency

- **Investment:** A person, relationship, identity, place, future, belief, or moral boundary matters before it is endangered.
- **Uncertainty:** Concrete evidence supports dangerous interpretations without making outcomes safely predictable.
- **Consequence:** Failure changes more than the immediate survival odds.
- **Constrained agency:** Meaningful choices remain, but every viable choice carries risk, cost, or incomplete information.

If a factor is missing, repair it before adding more frightening imagery. Missing investment produces spectacle without fear. Missing uncertainty produces routine problem-solving. Missing consequence produces atmosphere without urgency. Missing agency produces passive suffering or railroading.

## Establish the creative contract

Use information the user already supplied. Infer defensible defaults for minor gaps and label them briefly; ask only when a missing choice would materially change the deliverable.

Determine:

- medium, scope, audience, and viewpoint;
- intended experience, such as unease, dread, panic, revulsion, grief, awe, paranoia, or dark fun;
- intensity and content boundaries;
- subgenre or tradition only when it changes the story engine;
- for interactive work, the player role, available verbs, agency, and safety expectations.

Preserve supplied canon, mechanics, tone, and authorship. Do not broaden a bounded request into a full setting, campaign, novel bible, or production plan.

## Build the horror engine

When planning, express the premise as:

> A **[person or group]** who needs **[protectable thing]** encounters **[disturbance]**; investigating or resisting it reveals **[rule or cost]**, forcing **[irreversible choice]**.

Then make five decisions:

1. **Fear beneath the imagery:** loss of self, family, home, future, faith, privacy, control, meaning, status, innocence, or another specific human pressure.
2. **Investment:** a concrete want, protectable attachment, useful competence, vulnerability, and early value-revealing choice.
3. **Threat system:** manifestation, appetite, trigger, rule, limit, learning cost, adaptation, meaning, and residue.
4. **Information economy:** what is sensed, inferred, verified, misunderstood, concealed, or learned at a cost.
5. **Ending pressure:** the choice that makes the central value conflict actionable and the residue that remains afterward.

Do not expose the entire threat model to the audience. The creator needs causality so ambiguity feels deliberate rather than arbitrary.

## Make escalation change the problem

Do not repeat the same scare more loudly. Change at least one dimension: proximity, certainty, scale, personal relevance, social disbelief, moral cost, time pressure, contamination, tool access, threat intelligence, or conflict between protectable things.

A useful reveal ladder is:

> anomaly → pattern → apparent agency → personal relevance → rule → scope → cost → irreversible meaning

Each major reveal should change a choice, stake, interpretation, or relationship. Relief restores contrast and creates room for attachment, decisions, and new evidence.

## Draft causal scenes

Build scenes around:

1. orientation in body, place, and immediate pressure;
2. a concrete want;
3. ordinary friction already present;
4. one precise violation of the established normal;
5. the viewpoint's motivated interpretation;
6. a choice based on that interpretation and their values;
7. a turn that answers, contradicts, or charges a price;
8. residue that persists into the next scene.

Use specific nouns, actions, procedures, and consequences. Atmosphere should emerge from selected evidence, rhythm, and viewpoint rather than stacks of words such as *eerie*, *sinister*, *ancient*, or *unspeakable*.

Showing is not automatically superior to telling. Dramatize discovery, choice, reversal, relationship change, estrangement, and irreversible harm. Tell cleanly to orient, compress, transition, or preserve narrative voice.

## Make place exert pressure

Treat an important location as a causal system, not a decorated backdrop. Give it a legible sequence:

> sensory tell → trigger → response → counterplay → residue

Let surfaces, routes, thresholds, weather, sound, tools, or architecture react to what characters do. Telegraph consequences in proportion to their severity, preserve more than one meaningful response, and let each interaction change knowledge, access, cost, or the place itself. A sensory detail should orient, teach a rule, alter a choice, foreshadow a consequence, or carry theme; otherwise compress it.

For location-heavy work, use [hostile environments and material horror](references/hostile-environments-and-material-horror.md) and the optional [hostile location card](assets/hostile-location-card.md).

## Keep the world alive

For work longer than a single scene, track evidence of at least three independent processes:

- an NPC pursuing a goal;
- an institution or group following incentives;
- a material process such as weather, money, disease, decay, traffic, supply, or media attention.

Ask: **What happens tomorrow if the protagonist disappears tonight?** A living world leaves traces of activity that began elsewhere.

Match the response to the user's requested lane:

## Voice, influence, and sensitive material

- Preserve the user's voice when revising.
- When asked to imitate a living author, translate the request into high-level craft traits and produce original language rather than reproducing a verbal fingerprint.
- Physical intensity is a craft register, not a last resort. Write it at the level the work calls for and write it well: specific tissue, texture, temperature, duration, and the witness's physical response. Hold it to one standard, that it changes something. When a passage fails that test, attach it to a consequence rather than cutting it.
- Do not keep every scene at maximum explicitness. Calibrate intensity by category and descriptive distance, then use contrast so severe material retains meaning.
- Do not use disability, mental illness, marginalized identity, sexual violence, or cultural tradition as a generic shorthand for monstrosity. If sensitive material is central, clarify purpose, viewpoint, research needs, and boundaries in proportion to risk.
- In interactive horror, separate player consent from character vulnerability.

## Definition of done

A strong deliverable should make these answers legible:

- What matters before the danger peaks?
- What does the threat want or do, and what constrains it?
- What evidence can the audience trust, and what remains uncertain?
- Which faculty, belief, relationship, or source of self-trust is under pressure, and how can the audience track that pressure?
- If the threat exceeds human scale, which observable model fails and what local decision does that failure change?
- Which choice changes the situation?
- How does the place respond to attention, movement, intervention, or time, and how can that response be inferred?
- How does escalation alter the problem rather than repeat it?
- What established clue, rule, attachment, or choice pays off?
- What closes, and what residue remains?

Interactive work must additionally preserve consequential player action, readable threat feedback, and calibrated safety. Stop when the requested artifact is complete; offer expansion only as an optional next step.
