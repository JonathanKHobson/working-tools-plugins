# Narrative revision before line polish

Use when a scene, chapter, memoir passage or case narrative is structurally flat despite competent sentences, or when the user explicitly asks for a developmental pass. Do not apply a fiction diagnosis to a routine email, product explanation or technical document. Read the project's canon and the author's intended experience first; this reference does not authorize changing continuity or a settled theme.

## Identify the story's actual choice

Describe what the reader should know, feel or question before the passage and what changes afterward. Then inspect only the dimensions that could explain a failure:

| Dimension | Diagnostic question | Revision move when supported |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Agency and causality | Whose choice changes the situation, and what pressure makes it costly? | Put a decision or its consequence on the page; do not add an arbitrary action beat. |
| Time | Does a jump, repetition or pause help the reader interpret events? | Move or compress a beat to reveal the needed cause at the right moment. |
| Revelation | What does each character and the reader know at this point? | Give the reader an observable clue while preserving a deliberate secret. |
| Perspective | Does the POV know or conclude more than it reasonably can? | Return unsupported certainty to observation, inference or doubt. |
| Theme and motif | Which recurring detail earns meaning through change? | Reuse a specific object, gesture or cost in a new context; do not explain the moral. |
| Reader relationship | Is the reader invited to infer, dread, laugh or question at the intended distance? | Adjust what is shown, withheld or summarized without flattening voice. |

Do not optimize every dimension at once. Name the strongest structural issue and one or two viable moves, with the tradeoff each creates. Preserve parallel threads, unresolved grief, unreliable narration, unconventional chronology, and an ending without a lesson when these are intentional. A seeming contradiction may be a deliberate mystery; label it before proposing a fix.

Example: In a scene where a daughter sees her father's packed suitcase and says nothing, adding “She realized he would leave again and learned to accept it” may close an ambiguity the story needs. A structural revision could instead place the suitcase beside a repaired door, then let her decide whether to ask about it. The observable detail and choice sharpen the tension; whether the father is actually leaving remains unresolved. The particular image must come from project canon or be offered as a proposal, not silently added as fact.

After the structural choice, use `show-vs-tell-craft` only where dramatization or summary changes that effect, then line-edit. If the requested deliverable is revised prose, return it first and mention a canon or author decision only when it changed or remains material.
