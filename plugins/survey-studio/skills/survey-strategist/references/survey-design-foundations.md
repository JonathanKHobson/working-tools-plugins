# Survey Design Foundations

## Purpose

A survey is a standardized measurement instrument. Its value is not the number of questions it contains, but whether responses can support a defined decision for a defined population.

## The decision chain

For every item, complete this trace:

| Element | Required statement |
|---|---|
| Decision | What will someone choose or change? |
| Construct | What concept must be measured? |
| Observable indicator | What can the respondent validly report? |
| Question | What wording elicits that report? |
| Response representation | What values will be stored? |
| Analysis | How will those values be summarized or modeled? |
| Action threshold | What result changes the decision, if any? |

Delete questions that do not complete the chain. “It might be useful later” is not a measurement plan.

## Total survey error lens

A polished questionnaire can still produce poor evidence. Review at least five sources of error:

1. **Coverage error** — the sampling frame omits or overrepresents parts of the target population.
2. **Sampling error** — the selected sample differs from the population by chance or design.
3. **Nonresponse error** — participants and nonparticipants differ in ways related to the outcome.
4. **Measurement error** — wording, memory, context, mode, social desirability, or interface changes the response.
5. **Processing error** — logic, recodes, exports, cleaning, weighting, or analysis is wrong.

The best next improvement is the one that reduces the largest plausible error, not necessarily the one that makes the interface prettier.

## Minimum brief

Before drafting, define:

- decision owner and deadline;
- core problem and evidence gap;
- target population and eligibility;
- relevant experience and recall window;
- survey mode, trigger, language, and device context;
- required comparison groups;
- primary outcome and planned analysis;
- privacy, consent, incentive, and governance constraints;
- desired respondent burden;
- distribution channel and sampling frame;
- requested platform and deliverables.

## Design principles

### Ask only what respondents can know

Respondents can often report their recent behavior, perceptions, experiences, and circumstances. They are less reliable at explaining unconscious causes, predicting distant future behavior, estimating precise frequencies without records, or designing the correct product solution.

### Prefer concrete episodes

“During your most recent checkout…” is usually more answerable than “Generally, how is checkout?” Specify a timeframe that matches the construct and respondents’ memory.

### Minimize inferential distance

Ask about the closest observable indicator. To understand adoption barriers, ask what happened, what they tried, where they stopped, and what alternatives they used—not only whether they “value innovation.”

### Separate measurement from persuasion

A survey should not sell the product, justify a policy, teach the preferred answer, or pressure agreement. Put necessary context in neutral descriptive text and test whether it changes interpretation.

### Optimize the whole journey

Burden includes invitation clarity, eligibility questions, cognitive work, typing, scrolling, sensitive disclosure, inaccessible controls, repeated items, and uncertainty about progress. A five-item matrix may be harder than five simple pages.

## Evidence strength

Do not imply causation from a cross-sectional opinion survey. Label convenience samples and opt-in panels accurately. Report who was invited, who responded, field dates, mode, weighting, exclusions, item wording, and uncertainty appropriate to the design.

## Common failure modes

| Failure | Why it fails | Better move |
|---|---|---|
| Start with a list of desired questions | Produces irrelevant data and hidden construct gaps | Start with decisions and analysis |
| Replace interviews with a long open-text survey | Loses probing, context, and rapport | Interview first or triangulate |
| Ask the wrong audience | Measures non-users’ speculation instead of users’ experience | Define eligibility and sampling frame |
| Add demographics by default | Raises burden/privacy risk without analytical use | Collect only needed segmentation |
| Use one satisfaction score as diagnosis | Gives direction without cause | Add behavior/context and one diagnostic open end |
| Launch without pretesting | Treats researcher interpretation as universal | Cognitive pretest with target-like respondents |
| Analyze after seeing results | Encourages cherry-picking and moving thresholds | Prespecify primary analyses |

## Completion test

A stranger should be able to state: who the instrument is for, what decision it informs, why each question exists, what each coded value means, which paths respondents see, and what evidence would change the decision.
