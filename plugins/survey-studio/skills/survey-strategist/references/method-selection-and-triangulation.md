# Method Selection and Triangulation

## Choose based on the evidence gap

| Need | Strong starting method | Survey role |
|---|---|---|
| Estimate prevalence, distribution, or subgroup differences | Probability or carefully framed sample survey | Primary |
| Track a stable metric over time | Repeated survey with controlled wording/mode | Primary |
| Discover unknown problems, motives, language, or context | Interviews, observation, diary, support-log review | Follow-up quantification |
| Understand task breakdowns | Moderated/unmoderated usability test, telemetry | Post-task measure or recruitment |
| Test causal effect | Experiment or quasi-experiment | Measure outcomes/covariates |
| Prioritize alternatives | Choice experiment, MaxDiff, ranking with design expertise | Primary when assumptions fit |
| Collect required administrative information | Form/workflow | Not research unless analyzed as such |

## Survey–interview boundary

A survey is appropriate when:

- constructs and answer categories are sufficiently understood;
- standardized comparison matters;
- respondents can answer without probing;
- the sample and mode can reach the relevant population;
- analysis can be planned before collection.

Use interviews or contextual methods when:

- the problem space is still unknown;
- “why,” process, meaning, contradiction, or emotion is central;
- terminology and response categories are not yet known;
- the experience is complex, rare, sensitive, or difficult to recall;
- follow-up questions depend heavily on prior answers.

Open-ended survey questions do not recreate an interview. They generally produce shorter, less contextual responses and cannot clarify ambiguity in real time.

## Triangulation patterns

### Discover → measure

1. Interview or observe to identify problems and respondent language.
2. Convert recurring constructs into answerable items.
3. Pretest categories and wording.
4. Survey to estimate distribution and compare groups.

### Measure → explain

1. Detect a metric shift or subgroup difference.
2. Recruit survey respondents for follow-up interviews with consent.
3. Explore mechanisms and edge cases.
4. Use findings to revise hypotheses rather than retroactively “prove” them.

### Behavior + perception

Combine telemetry or records with a survey when behavior is observable but intent, effort, confidence, or unmet need is not. Join only with clear consent, minimization, and an appropriate identifier strategy.

### Experiment + experience

Use behavioral outcomes as primary evidence and a brief survey to measure manipulation checks, perceived ease, trust, or explanation. Avoid making self-reported preference the sole outcome when behavior is available.

## Decision rubric

Score each candidate method from 1–5 on:

- construct fit;
- population reach;
- ability to probe;
- standardization;
- causal identification;
- ecological validity;
- burden and sensitivity;
- time/cost;
- analysis skill required;
- privacy/operational risk.

Choose the portfolio that resolves the highest-risk uncertainty. “Cheapest” is not a method justification if it cannot answer the question.

## Claims discipline

- A survey can describe respondents; representativeness depends on design and execution.
- Correlation and stated reasons are not automatically causal mechanisms.
- Stated intention is not guaranteed future behavior.
- High response count does not repair biased coverage or wording.
- Mixed methods are not automatically stronger; the methods must address complementary uncertainty and be integrated deliberately.
