# Question Writing

## A strong item is answerable, interpreted consistently, and analytically useful

### Write one construct at a time

Bad: “How easy and satisfying was setup?”

Better:
- “How easy or difficult was it to set up the device?”
- Ask satisfaction separately only if it supports a distinct analysis.

### Bound the recall period

Bad: “How often do you use the feature?”

Better: “In the past 30 days, on how many days did you use the feature?”

Match the window to event frequency and memory. “Ever” measures lifetime exposure, not current behavior.

### Use respondent language

Avoid internal jargon, acronyms, legalistic prose, and product taxonomy respondents may not recognize. Define necessary terms before the item without implying the preferred answer.

### Ask about observable experience

Bad: “Why did you abandon checkout?” assumes a single conscious cause.

Better sequence:
1. “What happened the last time you tried to check out?”
2. “Which of these, if any, did you experience?”
3. “What was the main reason you did not complete checkout?”

### Keep neutral balance

Bad: “How helpful was our improved support?”

Better: “How helpful or unhelpful was the support you received?”

Mention both directions or use neutral labels when a construct can plausibly be positive or negative.

## Bias patterns

| Pattern | Example | Repair |
|---|---|---|
| Leading | “How much did the new design improve…” | Ask whether/how the experience changed |
| Loaded | “Do you support protecting children by…” | Separate value claim from policy item |
| Assumptive | “Which premium features do you use?” | First ask whether any are used |
| Double-barreled | “fast and reliable” | split constructs |
| Double negative | “Do you disagree that it should not…” | write direct positive syntax |
| Absolute | “always/never” without justification | bounded frequency categories |
| Prestige/authority | “Experts agree…” | remove source cue unless experimentally manipulated |
| Social desirability | “Do you responsibly recycle?” | normalize range and ask concrete behavior |
| Hypothetical certainty | “Would you buy…” | present realistic conditions; interpret cautiously |
| Knowledge test disguised as opinion | technical premise in question | measure knowledge separately or provide neutral facts |

## Brevity and comprehension

Aim for one concise sentence, but do not omit necessary timeframe, object, or definition merely to shorten it. Put optional explanation in help text. Keep response labels grammatically compatible with the stem.

## Open-ended items

Use open text when:

- possible categories are not yet known;
- exact wording, story, or diagnostic detail matters;
- a reason is needed after a structured rating;
- a rare issue cannot be represented efficiently.

Make it optional unless the response is operationally necessary. Ask one focused prompt, not “Any other comments?” by default. Example: “What is the one change that would have made this task easier?”

Do not ask for sensitive stories in a generic text box without warning respondents not to include names, account numbers, health details, or other unnecessary identifiers.

## Knowledge and factual questions

Give “not sure” when uncertainty is legitimate. Do not force guessing. For numeric quantities, decide whether exact number, bounded categories, or record lookup is realistic.

## Translation-ready writing

Avoid idioms, humor that changes meaning, metaphors, culture-bound examples, ambiguous pronouns, and embedded wordplay. Maintain a term glossary and document the intended construct—not just the English sentence.

## Item review checklist

- Is the respondent eligible and able to know the answer?
- Is there exactly one object, construct, and timeframe?
- Could two reasonable people interpret key terms differently?
- Does wording imply a preferred answer?
- Is recall realistic?
- Do the choices fit the grammar and cover plausible answers?
- Can a refusal or inapplicable response be expressed honestly?
- Is the item required for a named decision or analysis?
- Will it translate and work on mobile/assistive technology?
- Has it been cognitively pretested?
