# Survey Types

Survey “type” may refer to purpose, timing, population, mode, or design. Name all relevant dimensions rather than assuming one label determines the method.

## By purpose

| Type | Primary purpose | Typical content | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Problem discovery survey | Quantify known candidate problems after qualitative discovery | Recent behavior, workarounds, frequency, impact, open diagnostic | Asking respondents to invent solutions |
| Audience/needs survey | Characterize relevant segments and contexts | Eligibility, jobs, behaviors, constraints, needs | Over-segmentation and stereotype proxies |
| Concept test | Evaluate comprehension, relevance, differentiation, concerns | Neutral concept exposure, comprehension, ratings, reasons | Selling in the stimulus; order bias |
| Product feedback | Diagnose experience with a product or feature | Task/feature use, ease, success, problems, outcome | Generic satisfaction without context |
| Transactional/triggered survey | Capture a specific recent event | Event outcome, effort, issue, resolution | Trigger bias and repeated invitations |
| Relationship survey | Periodic overall experience or loyalty | Overall outcomes, drivers, open comment | Recall vagueness; overreliance on one score |
| Customer satisfaction survey | Measure satisfaction with defined object/window | Satisfaction scale plus drivers and issue resolution | Scale inconsistency and benchmark misuse |
| Employee pulse | Monitor a small set of workplace constructs | Climate, workload, enablement, safety, open comment | Re-identification and low psychological safety |
| Engagement/census survey | Broader organizational assessment | Validated domains, subgroup analysis | Survey fatigue; action gap |
| Screener | Determine eligibility or quotas | Essential inclusion/exclusion criteria | Revealing desired answers; unnecessary sensitive data |
| Intake/application form | Collect information needed to deliver a service or decide eligibility | Identity, contact, structured facts, documents | Treating required administration as voluntary research |
| Registration/RSVP | Enroll participants and gather logistics | Contact, attendance, accommodations | Collecting “nice to have” fields |
| Post-task usability | Measure a just-completed task | completion, ease, confidence, issue | Asking too late or without task context |
| Diary/experience sampling | Repeated snapshots close to experience | event, context, emotion, behavior | Attrition, reactivity, notification burden |
| Poll | Rapid measurement of a small set of public opinions | a few standardized items | Weak sampling/coverage disguised by precision |
| Evaluation survey | Assess program/service implementation and outcomes | exposure, delivery, perceived/actual outcomes | Attribution without comparison design |
| Academic questionnaire | Operationalize research constructs | validated/adapted scales, covariates | Undocumented modifications and multiplicity |

## By time design

- **Cross-sectional:** one measurement window; useful for a snapshot, limited for change and causal order.
- **Repeated cross-sectional:** new samples at multiple times; tracks population-level movement if wording, mode, and coverage remain comparable.
- **Panel/longitudinal:** same respondents over time; supports individual change analysis but introduces attrition, conditioning, and identity/privacy needs.
- **Pre/post:** measures before and after an intervention; without a comparison group, change may have other causes.
- **Event-triggered:** close to a transaction or behavior; improves recall but covers only triggered people/events.

## By mode

Web, mobile app, email-embedded, SMS, paper, telephone, in-person interviewer, kiosk, IVR, and mixed-mode surveys produce different coverage, presentation, interviewer, and privacy effects. Do not change mode mid-trend without evaluating comparability.

## Choosing scope

Prefer the narrowest type that resolves the decision. A one-question intercept can be appropriate when the only need is a timely diagnostic and the trigger is precise. A long “everything survey” is rarely comprehensive; it is usually a set of compromised studies sharing one invitation.
